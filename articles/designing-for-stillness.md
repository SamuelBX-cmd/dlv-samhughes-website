I was recently in Japan, taking a brief break and as always wanting to fuel a bit of curiosity and discover something new.

A friend suggested a visit to Teshima Art Museum, it's a small island in the Seto Inland Sea, a ferry ride from the mainland. I didn't know much about it, I went in with zero expectations but also happy for a day long adventure.

The museum is barely a building. A curved concrete shell, open to the sky. No art on the walls. No signage. No soundtrack. Just a vast, hushed space — and tiny beads of water emerging slowly from the floor, finding each other, pooling, moving.

You remove your shoes at the entrance. You lower your voice instinctively. You sit, eventually, because standing feels like you're waiting for something that isn't coming.

It's uncomfortable at first. Genuinely.

We're not used to experiences that don't perform for us. That don't fill the silence or tell us what to feel. Teshima just holds you there — until something quietly shifts.

That discomfort, I realised, was the design.

It stayed with me long after I left the island. And it got me thinking about how rarely brands are willing to do this. To resist the urge to fill every moment. To trust that a slower pace, or even a little friction, might be exactly what makes an experience memorable.

And that memorability matters more than we often measure. Return on experience isn't just a feel-good metric — it's the difference between a customer who transacts and one who comes back, tells others, and genuinely connects with what you stand for. Teshima has no loyalty programme. No app. No follow-up email. Yet I've told more people about that museum than almost any brand experience I've had this year.

The best experiences aren't always the smoothest ones. Sometimes they're the ones that made you stop — and actually feel something.

What would your brand look like if you designed for stillness instead of stimulation?
