Rimowa’s RE-CRAFTED program is a fantastic example of embracing imperfection and permanence. Their refurbished luggage proudly wears the scuffs and scratches of past journeys. It’s not just about sustainability—it’s about storytelling and preserving the heritage embedded in each piece. This approach reframes value not as ‘newness’ but as *history you can hold*, turning wear and tear into badges of honour. In some ways it's a fast track into signalling the status of a well-travelled life.

The concept of permanence is not entirely new to luxury. Brands like Hermès and Patek Philippe have long positioned their products as generational investments through repair programs and strategic campaigns. Similarly, manufactured imperfection has been used by brands like Levis for decades.

What's interesting in a time where luxury is being constantly redefined by cultural and technological shifts, there is a blending of traditional values with innovation. Authenticity and provenance gain renewed importance through digital passports and blockchain technology. Our recent collaborations with eBay and the Volte exemplify how partnerships can champion product longevity, while simultaneously addressing luxury's accessibility through rental and resale.

I imagine an extension on the RE-CRAFTED program with a suitcase that has a blockchain-enabled digital passport, detailing its repair history and ownership chain, that could be rented to occasional travellers showing the unique journey that has enriched its character. This integration of technology reinforces a new kind of premium: *transparent longevity*.

In a landscape dominated by rapid change and disposability, genuine luxury isn’t about constantly seeking the new; it’s about establishing enduring systems and meaningful experiences. By integrating circular economy principles with technology to assure provenance and authenticity, luxury brands reaffirm their core identity: permanence, heritage, and the inherent beauty of time.

As luxury increasingly embraces longevity, circularity, and craftsmanship, it prompts us to consider what implications this holds for technology-centric brands like Apple, Oura, or Vertu.
