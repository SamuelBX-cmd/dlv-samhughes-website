We have all been using AI to outsource the emotional and practical labour of decisions. For everything from flights to insurance, the past couple of years have seen an acceleration that has shifted behaviours from a novelty to the norm. The impact of this is seen in a recent study where 185 documented purchase decisions found 74 per cent of consumers chose whatever the AI ranked first as its top pick. This convenience has very real consequences for brands. Every time those queries are made, three choices show up. Hundreds don’t. What’s more, most brands will have no idea whether they even showed up or not.

Brand strategy now has two audiences with opposing requirements. The first is the human, still ultimately responsible for the decision, driven by emotion, habit, and years of behaviour. The second is the machine that increasingly shapes what the human sees before the decision is made.

Previously, we lived in a world where the portion of preference driven by brand relied on distinctiveness, emotion and memory. This still works well, albeit only on people. AI, on the other hand, is driven by verifiable data and precise language, which can often be in contrast to building a deeper and more engaging connection.

In the 20 or so years I have been involved in building brands, the emergence of an entirely new synthetic audience is changing how people behave in ways I have never seen before. These two audiences must be won differently. And in this new arrangement, the machine’s preference is no longer just one signal among many, it’s the filter through which the human’s choice is shaped.

For Australian brands, this is acute. The systems shaping these decisions are trained largely on US or global data, and local category leaders are not yet legible to these systems by default. It’s a familiar problem made sharper. The visibility gap Amazon created for Australian retailers is now being widened by an AI layer that doesn’t see them at all.

Humans want meaning; machines want structure. The connection a customer has built with a brand over years can thin out quickly when an AI agent sits in the middle, optimising the instruction while drawing down the emotional credit the brand took decades to earn. Humans are looking for distinctiveness; a machine rewards clarity.

A brand with a clear point of view, a distinctive voice or a unique asset stands in contrast to the need for a clear data point to reference, and a message that is more legible but less recognisable.

Humans forgive ambiguity; machines punish it. Trust between people is built in the space where things are assumed in good faith, what philosophers call the principle of charity, and it shows up most clearly in repair. When something goes wrong, a vague acknowledgment that the moment was hard for the customer often does more than a precise diagnosis. The machine reads only the precision. Everything else is noise, including the gestures that repaired the relationship.

For brand and business leaders, this is not only about optimisation, it’s about redefining how your brand operates across two audiences by deliberately shaping a more holistic experience, and understanding what and who matters most across each moment.

Balancing these shifts of preference is key: First, understand that the version of your brand that survives the AI layer is the version the AI can describe. The idiosyncrasies and distinctive elements outside of name, pricing and key features will be stripped away. The brands that hold their distinctiveness will accept that the AI layer represents them imperfectly.

Second, understand what matters most, to whom, and when. Your website is no longer a point of entry for a purchase: by the time someone has landed on the site, the decision has already been made. Making those journeys easy but memorable is key to building a deeper connection.

Thirdly, we are now living in a time when offline experiences matter more than ever. Physical presence and tangible moments, particularly among younger audiences shifting from experiential retail to a tactile renaissance, mean the digital ecosystem with its AI layer isn’t the beginning nor end of a journey but another part of a deeper experience.

The next move for brands is not to compete to be a part of the answer, but to change the question being asked. The goal is no longer about ranking as high as possible in a response to “Get me tickets to Berlin in October,” but about building enough emotional currency to change the question to “Get me the best-priced Qantas tickets to Berlin in October.”

Brand isn’t dying; it’s being given a new lease of life and asked to do more. Legibility is the price of entry. Preference is the prize. Confuse the two and you become part of a transaction: opaque to the customer, reductive to the brand. But build your brand for both, and AI works for the brand instead of looking through it. The brand experience will be positive, but your new audience still won’t smile.
