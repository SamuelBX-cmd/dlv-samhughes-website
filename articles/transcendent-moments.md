Recently, I had the privilege of seeing Max Richter live in concert. One comment my friend made about the experience stuck with me, it was transcendent. From beginning to end, the audience was captivated, and with a ban on filming, there were no distractions from flashing screens—only the carefully designed lighting of the stage.

Reflecting on this, in a world where minimalism and ease dominate, immersive experiences demand something different. They ask us to participate, to feel, and to co-create. From the multi-sensory brilliance of installations by studios like TeamLab and Marshmallow Laserfeast to the deeply affecting compositions of Max Richter, we see that immersion is not just about spectacle, it’s about execution.

Time is a crucial component. Immersion is not passive; it unfolds, engaging us fully in moments where disbelief is suspended and reality bends. When done well, time can be used as a tool to create anticipation and control the journey, setting a pace that transports us somewhere else. Whether it is a retail environment, an experiential brand activation, or a theatrical performance, a well-executed experience that considers time and multi-sensory elements has the power to stay with us long after it ends, creating peaks in our memory that define how we recall an experience and deepen the connection.

But when immersion fails, the disappointment can be heightened. Because these experiences command our complete attention, any misstep is amplified; failing to meet our expectations pulls us out of the moment. A hyper-exaggerated example is the Wonka World in Glasgow, famous for all the wrong reasons.

For brands, this is an opportunity. A well-crafted immersive moment deepens emotional engagement, strengthens brand recall, and leaves an indelible mark. The challenge is ensuring that the execution matches the ambition.

What is the most immersive experience you have ever had? And what made it work?
